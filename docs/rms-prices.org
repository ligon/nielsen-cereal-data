#+title: Homescan vs RMS prices: what differs, and why
#+date: 2026-10-19

Requested by EL after D4 and the theta-gap check (2026-10-19): "For
prices: what are the differences?  Do they depend on coupons, reporting,
...?  Are there important differences in the two price sources?"  Sections
1-5 are documented facts, with page citations.  Section 6 is measurement
(=src/rms_prices.py=).  Sections 7 and 8 are interpretation and open
questions, and say so.  No retailer is named; "the largest outside chain"
is the biggest Homescan cereal retailer that does not report to Kilts RMS.

Sources and how they are cited:
- CP: /Kilts NielsenIQ Consumer Panel Dataset Manual/, 2004-2024 edition
  (Kilts =Reference_Documentation.tgz=, consumer-panel bundle).  Its
  "Universal" sections and "Batch 1 (2004-2020)" layouts cover the classic
  years.  Page numbers are the manual's own.
- RMS: /Kilts NielsenIQ Retail Scanner Dataset Manual/ 2006-2020, updated
  04/15/2022 (scanner bundle).  The 2021-onward manual (2024 edition)
  repeats the movement-file notes cited here (its pp. 6-7, 16-17).
- ELN: Einav, Leibtag and Nevo, "Recording discrepancies in Nielsen

* 0. Short answer

Most important first.  Measured unless marked otherwise.

1. *Coverage is the main difference.*  Only 46.6% of the panel's
   2006-2024 cereal dollars are spent at retailers that report to RMS
   (42.9% of all module-1344 dollars).  At those retailers Homescan and RMS
   prices agree in level: mean d = log p_RMS - log p_HS = 0.001, against
   D4's 0.052.  Purchases elsewhere cost 0.093 log points less on the same
   cells.  So D4's gap is entirely the uncovered half of Homescan.
   Across the 45 brands, the D4 gap correlates -0.61 with the brand's
   covered share of dollars.  Restricted to covered retailers, the brand
   gaps shrink from 0.010-0.111 to -0.029-0.050.
2. *Coverage also explains much of the low two-way correlation.*  Net of
   product and (t, m) effects, the correlation with RMS is 0.474 for
   covered-only Homescan cells and 0.225 for uncovered-only cells, on the
   same 182,833 cells.  The full panel gives 0.446 on those cells, and a
   random split of trips the size of the covered half gives 0.358.  For
   cells with at least 25 transactions, covered cells reach 0.68-0.70 and
   placebo cells 0.36-0.45.  Uncovered cells peak at 10-24 transactions
   and then fall as they get thicker (0.28, 0.23, 0.17, 0.12).  Correlation that falls with more data is not
   sampling noise: those stores price differently, and RMS does not see
   them.
3. *Where a store reports, the two sources record the same price.*
   Documented: at stores whose POS data NielsenIQ receives, the Homescan
   price is not what the panelist paid.  It is the store's volume-weighted
   average price for that UPC that week, the same object as the RMS price
   (CP pp. 9, 20; RMS pp. 15-16; ELN pp. 2, 4).  Measured: on (store, UPC,
   week) matches, the Homescan unit price equals RMS price / prmult to the
   cent for 86.7% of records in 2008, 78.0% in 2014 and 70.2% in 2022.
4. *Even at reporting stores, Homescan cells add variation RMS lacks:
   which weeks, stores and UPCs the few sample households bought.*  In
   2014, pricing the panel's store-linked purchases at the exact RMS
   store-week price moves the two-way correlation with RMS only from 0.35
   to 0.37.  Pricing them at the RMS store-quarter average for the same
   UPC raises it to 0.55; the rest is store and UPC mix.  2008 and 2022
   show the same pattern.
5. *Coupons are not a difference as the panels stand.*  Both prices are
   gross of manufacturer coupons (documented): the panel uses
   =total_price_paid=, which is before coupons, and RMS excludes
   manufacturer coupons.  Coupons are 6.2% of gross Homescan dollars,
   ranging from 1.9% to 9.5% by brand.  Netting them out of Homescan moves
   it away from RMS: the gap rises to 0.106 and the two-way correlation
   falls to 0.378 (0.403 on covered cells).  So coupons would add
   within-(t, m) relative-price variation that RMS lacks, but only if
   someone netted them, and the panel does not.
6. *Deal flags do not matter for agreement.*  =deal_flag_uc= marks a deal
   as the panelist perceived it; 41.4% of dollars carry one.  Keeping only
   non-deal purchases lowers the two-way correlation (0.352).
7. *Loyalty cards affect both sources the same way at covered stores*
   (follows from CP p. 20 and RMS p. 16, note F; ELN pp. 11-12).  The weekly average mixes card and non-card buyers,
   so both sources overstate what a card-holder paid.  This does not
   create disagreement between the two sources.

* 1. The two price concepts (documented)

Both files' =prices= are revenue / ounces per (t, m, j) (D4 section 2).

- Homescan (the panel): revenue = sum of =total_price_paid= over the
  sample households' purchase records (AM:636-645), unprojected.
  =total_price_paid= is "Total price paid for all units, before
  discounts".  =coupon_value= is "Total discount for amount due to
  coupon".  "To calculate the final price paid by panelist, subtract
  =coupon_value= from =total_price_paid=" (CP p. 27, Purchases file and
  Note 1; the same for 2021+ on pp. 40-41; FAQ 24, p. 62).  The panel uses
  =total_price_paid= as is, so its price is *gross of coupons*.
- RMS: revenue = sum over store-weeks of units x price / prmult.  =price=
  is "The volume weighted average price of the product for that week"
  (RMS p. 15).  "NielsenIQ uses the volume weighted price of all sales for
  that good in that week" (p. 16, note G).  It is a weekly average over
  transactions, not the modal price or the shelf price.  Multi-unit deals:
  "3 for $1" is prmult = 3, price = $1.00, and "The unit price would be
  price/prmult" (p. 6; p. 16, note E).  Sales tax is typically excluded
  (note H).  Free goods are coded at 1 cent, because zero prices are not
  allowed (pp. 17-18, note N).
- What the RMS price includes (RMS p. 16, note F): "Retailer discounts
  and specials (e.g. discounts associated with the use of a retailer's
  loyalty card or coupons) are factored into the weighted average weekly
  price.  Manufacturer coupons are not factored into the weighted average
  weekly price."
- NielsenIQ edits RMS: prices are changed in under ~1% of cases (a
  quarter of those are outliers re-imputed from history, three quarters
  are unit-basis alignments).  Under ~3% of store-weeks are wholly
  imputed.  No flag marks either (RMS p. 16, note D).
- Panelist-typed prices: the instruction is to "enter the price paid
  (prior to any coupons or deals)" (CP p. 20; FAQ 21, p. 62).  The manual
  does not say whether "deals" means the shelf price before a temporary
  reduction, or only before a deduction at the register.

* 2. Coupons (documented)

- Homescan records the panelist's coupon as =coupon_value=, a dollar
  discount on the record (CP p. 9, item 2d; p. 21; p. 27).  The manual
  does not say whether it covers only manufacturer coupons or retailer
  coupons too.
- RMS has no coupon field.  Retailer coupons are inside the weekly price;
  manufacturer coupons are not (RMS p. 16, note F).
- Both panels, as built, are therefore gross of manufacturer coupons.
  Netting coupons out of Homescan would move it away from RMS's concept,
  unless =coupon_value= is mostly retailer coupons, which RMS already
  nets.
- ELN (p. 15, fn. 14): their price regressions (who pays less for the same
  item) are "qualitatively similar" with prices net of coupons.

* 3. Deals (documented)

- Homescan: =deal_flag_uc= = 1 if "the item was perceived by the consumer
  to be on a 'deal'" (CP p. 9, item 2c; p. 21; layout p. 27).  It records
  the panelist's perception, not a price fact.  On loyalty cards: "If the
  panelist has a frequent shopper card and is aware that a particular item
  was discounted for card members, they can indicate this as a deal but
  are not required to do so" (CP p. 21).  A zero =total_price_paid= with
  a deal flag is a valid "free goods" price; without a flag, NielsenIQ
  advises treating it as missing (FAQ 22, p. 62).
- RMS has no deal flag.  Feature and display flags exist only for an
  "audited" subset of stores (RMS pp. 6, 16-17, notes J-L), and there is
  no temporary-price-reduction indicator (note M).  Multi-unit deals are
  coded through prmult (note E).

* 4. Coverage and the store link (documented)

- Homescan covers purchases "from any outlet" (CP p. 9).  RMS covers only
  the stores of retailers that share with Kilts: "not every NielsenIQ
  retail cooperator has agreed to share their scanner data with the Kilts
  Center" (RMS p. 7).  So there are three kinds of store, not two:
  1. stores in Kilts RMS;
  2. NielsenIQ POS cooperators that do not share with Kilts;
  3. stores NielsenIQ has no POS data for.
- Question (d) of the request: trips carry both =retailer_code= (the
  chain, at banner level; CP p. 25) and =store_code_uc=.  The manual
  describes =store_code_uc= as "ID code for each individual store.  This
  store_code_uc can be used to link consumer panel data with the retail
  scanner data.  Note that a value of zero represents 'missing'" (CP
  p. 26; also p. 9; RMS p. 10, note A).  "Store code and store zip are
  provided only for a subset of trips" (CP p. 9).
- RMS =stores= carry a =retailer_code= "inferred from the Consumer Panel
  Dataset" (RMS p. 8).  It "is the same as the retailer_code field in the
  Consumer Panel dataset", and it is blank for a store with no panel
  transactions (RMS pp. 10-11, notes A and C).  In the classic years, 42%
  of store-years have no retailer_code for that reason (RMS p. 23).
- AUDIT-2022 (2022 only): 83.6% of Homescan cereal dollars are on trips
  with a store code.  38.4% are at an RMS-2022 store by store code, and
  42.8% at an RMS retailer_code.  The single largest uncovered chain is
  21.1%.  138 of RMS's 144 chains appear in the trips.  Section 6.2
  reproduces the 38.4% and 42.8% exactly.

* 5. Homescan prices imputed from store data (documented)

This is the point that decides whether the two sources share a price
concept.

- CP p. 9 (data entry sequence): "Price - entered only if the product was
  purchased at a store that doesn't provide NielsenIQ with retail scanner
  data.  Otherwise, the price is filled in by NielsenIQ."
- CP p. 20 (Price Determination): "if the outlet shopped is a retailer for
  which NielsenIQ receives electronic point of sale (POS) data, the price
  prompt is bypassed.  In this case, the price reported is an average
  weighted price for the item that week in that particular store."  It
  goes on: "For certain harder to recruit-maintain demographics, panelists
  are not required to enter prices for any retailers.  These prices are
  then imputed using POS data as well as other sources of data (prices
  ranges, median prices from other panelists etc)", and "NielsenIQ does
  not distinguish prices entered by the panelist from those that are
  imputed".  FAQ 21 (pp. 61-62) repeats this.
- RMS p. 15 (note B) and p. 16 (note C): "In most cases for panel data,
  panelists do not enter prices for retailers for which NielsenIQ receives
  scanner data.  Prices are ascribed from the scanner data for the
  specific store a panelist shopped in the reported week.  As such, prices
  should generally be consistent for scanning data and panel data."  There
  are two exceptions: retailers where NielsenIQ does not ascribe (the
  panelists enter prices), and late transmissions, where "the imputed
  price is based on the following week's scanning data".  Note B also
  warns that a retailer's 7-day week need not end on Saturday.
- ELN validated 2004 Homescan against one retailer's transaction records.
  They write: "when available, Nielsen uses store-level (average) prices
  instead of the actual price paid by the household" (p. 2; mechanism
  p. 4).  The two prices disagree in about half the cases, with
  correlation 0.88; the error is 21.8% of the variance of the Homescan
  price.  Where no loyalty-card discount was offered, the error is 8.5% of
  the variance and the correlation is 0.96.  Where a card discount
  applied, Homescan overstates the card-holder's price (mean error above
  14%), because the weekly average mixes card and non-card buyers
  (pp. 11-12).  At that retailer, cards were used in 75-80% of
  transactions, and about 60% of transacted items carried a card discount
  (p. 11).

Consequence (documented): at a store whose POS data NielsenIQ receives,
the Homescan price for a store-UPC-week is the RMS price for that
store-UPC-week, not what the household paid.  This holds for every
NielsenIQ POS store, including those of chains that do not share with
Kilts.  So "outside RMS" does not mean "typed by the panelist".

* 6. Measurements

** 6.1 Method

=src/rms_prices.py hs= rebuilds the panel's (t, m, j) cells from the raw
purchase records with the panel builder's own helpers (AM imports: trips
on (trip_code_uc, panel_year), household DMA, quarter of purchase date,
CTL* dropped, 2021+ brand crosswalk, OZ only, the panel's 45 j and 65 m).
Check: the all-purchases rebuild reproduces =market_panel.parquet=
exactly on the 2006-2024 quarters.  It has the same 214,637 cells, a maximum relative price
difference of 0.0, and identical =n_transactions=.

Each purchase is then tagged by the RMS stores with module-1344 movement
in its calendar year (=diagnostics/stores_by_year=):
- /RMS store/: the trip's =store_code_uc= is one of those stores;
- /covered/: the trip's =retailer_code= is a chain of one of those stores
  (every store-linked purchase is also covered).

Cells are then re-aggregated from subsets of purchases, or with
=coupon_value= netted out.  Each set of cells is compared with
=market_panel_rms.parquet= using D4 section 2's statistics:
- corr of log p, pooled, within (t, m), and two-way (net of product and
  (t, m) fixed effects, alternating projections over that variant's own
  common cells);
- sd of the two-way residuals;
- d = log p_RMS - log p_HS.

/Placebo/ is a seeded random 46.6% of trips, the covered share of
dollars, with its complement, so that thinning is separated from coverage.

** 6.6 At RMS stores, why do cells still disagree?

The same store-linked, matched purchases are priced four ways and
aggregated to (t, m, j):
1. =hs=: the Homescan price;
2. =rms_week=: the matched RMS store-week price (same store, UPC and
   week);
3. =rms_storeqtr=: the RMS store-quarter average for that store and UPC
   (units-weighted over the quarter's weeks);
4. =rms=: the RMS cell price from =market_panel_rms=.

Step 1 to 2 is recording or imputation error; 2 to 3 is the week the
household bought in; 3 to 4 is which stores and UPCs (sizes) the sample
bought, against all shoppers in the DMA.  Two-way FE are fitted on these
cells (8,723-10,206 cells per year).

* 7. Which differences give one source residual price variation the other lacks (interpretation)

D4's two-way correlation of 0.40, plateauing near 0.5 in thick cells,
reads as a dollar-weighted mixture of two components.  The covered
component is about 47% of panel dollars; its prices correlate 0.47
with RMS, and about 0.7 in thick cells.  The uncovered component is
about 53% of panel dollars; it correlates 0.22, and less as cells get
thicker.  In order of importance, these are the differences that give
the panel residual (within product, within (t, m)) variation that RMS
lacks:

1. *Retail sector.*  Half of panel dollars are at stores RMS does not
   cover: the largest outside chain (about 15-17% of panel dollars), other
   chains with store codes, and stores without codes.  Their relative
   prices across brands within a market-quarter differ from those at RMS
   chains, and the difference is systematic, not sampling.  They are also
   9.3% cheaper on average, which is all of D4's level gap.
2. *Purchase timing.*  Within RMS stores, a panel cell prices the
   particular weeks its households bought in.  Deal weeks are
   over-represented (53% of covered dollars are deal-flagged).  RMS
   averages all weeks.  This is the largest within-store component
   (+0.15-0.18 in correlation, 2008-2022).
3. *Store and package-size mix of the sample.*  A cell holds a handful of
   households (median 17 transactions; 8 at covered retailers).  The
   stores and sizes they happen to buy shift the unit value per ounce.
4. *Coupons, if netted.*  They are brand-specific (1.9-9.5% of gross),
   with a two-way residual sd of 0.088 in log(1 - c), and RMS has nothing
   like them.  The panel as built does not net them, so they are not in
   D4's 0.40.
5. *Recording and imputation error.*  Real (13-30% of matched records are
   off by more than a cent) and concentrated in a few chains, but worth
   only 0.01-0.02 of correlation at the cell level.
6. *Minor:* household vs store DMA (2-3% of store-linked dollars),
   late-transmission next-week prices (1-2% of records), and NielsenIQ's
   RMS edits (<1%).

What RMS has and the panel lacks: all shoppers in all weeks at covered
stores.  That is why RMS's residual sd is lower (0.100 vs 0.128 on D4's
cells, and 0.07-0.09 vs 0.15-0.19 on the store-linked cells of 6.6).
RMS's quarter assignment by week_end (12-14 week quarters, D4) moves
quantities but not prices.

On theta (docs/rms-theta-gap.org; conjecture): the panel's extra residual
price variation is not classical noise around the RMS price.  It is
largely the price of a different retail sector, plus the households' own
choice of weeks.  So these numbers cannot sign its effect on homog's
theta, and they are consistent with the theta-gap note's finding that
thicker panel cells do not move the panel toward RMS.  The direct test,
homog on the panel rebuilt from covered purchases only, was run (EL,
2026-10-19; =docs/rms-theta-gap.org=, "Coverage test"): covered cells give
theta 0.71 and a random set of trips with the same dollar share 0.73,
against 1.22 for the full panel and 2.26 for RMS.  Coverage does not
explain the theta gap; thinning the panel's cells lowers its theta.  The
cells are variant =covered_gross= of
=Data/NielsenScanner/rms/prices/hs_cells.parquet= (group-only; =python
src/rms_prices.py hs --outdir DIR= regenerates it in about two minutes).

* 8. What is not established

- Whether Homescan prices at non-RMS stores are typed by panelists or
  imputed from NielsenIQ POS data at cooperators that do not share with
  Kilts.  The documented rule implies imputation at every POS store, but
  the probes in 6.7 cannot tell the two apart.
- Why 17 chains (25% of matched dollars) match to the cent less than half
  the time in 2014: the documented "panelists enter prices" exception,
  or reporting weeks that do not end on Saturday.
- *2021+ anomaly.*  In 2022, 7.7% of matched records have a Homescan
  price 1.8 or more times the RMS store-week price (2014: 1.0%; 2008:
  0.65%), and the mean r is 0.085.  prmult is 1 on every matched 2022 RMS
  row, and there are no zero-price Homescan records in 2021+.  This is why
  the covered-only mean d turns negative in 2021-2023 (-0.016 to
  -0.024, section 6.3).  A
  candidate cause is free-unit or multi-buy promotions that RMS counts
  in units but Homescan does not reflect in its price; another is a
  change in how NielsenIQ ascribes prices in the revised format.
  Neither is checked.
- Whether =coupon_value= includes retailer coupons, which RMS nets into
  price.  The manual says only "discount ... due to coupon".
- What "prior to any coupons or deals" means for prices panelists type
  (CP p. 20): shelf price before a temporary reduction, or the price
  paid before a register deduction.
- Why covered cells plateau near 0.7 rather than higher, beyond the
  timing and mix shown in 6.6.  Also why the Malt-O-Meal brands keep a
  0.05 gap even at covered retailers (package-size mapping, or bag sizes
  sold mainly through some chains, is a guess).
- The theta consequence beyond coverage.  Restricting the panel to
  covered purchases does not move theta toward RMS (section 7); what does
  separate the two sources' thetas is open.
